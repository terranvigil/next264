# Table, 2026-08-28: 1080p, and what the wall-clock ratio was hiding

The standing table has three CIF clips and three 720p clips, so it says nothing
about 1080p. This run adds four 1080p rows and then chases the discrepancy they
expose: goal 3 reads 0.96x on the six-clip table and 1.44x here. Both are real.
Thread occupancy is the difference.

Both encoders run as libraries in one ffmpeg process through
`scripts/ffboard.py`, driven by the `perf-comp-*` make targets, at matched CRF,
preset medium, median of 3 samples. Every cell waited for the 1-minute load
average to fall under 1.2 before it started. Repeats of the same cells on a busy
box came in within 0.02 of these, so the settle wait is cheap insurance rather
than a correction.

## The 1080p rows

Auto threads, as shipped, 15-second window. CRF 25 on our side, x264 solved onto
the rate we achieved.

| perseverance_1080p | 3741 | 25.34 | 24.19 | 1.44x | 1.46x | 16.1 / 15.8 | +0.84 | +0.3% |

Repeats of all four across the day sat inside 0.05, so a single 1080p row is
good to about half a tenth and no better.

The quality column is the good news. On each of the three natural-video clips
x264 had to drop more than a CRF step below ours to reach the rate we hit, and
we still scored ahead on VMAF-NEG at the same file size. The advantage tracks
how hard the content is, and bbb's clean CGI is the floor of it.

**NOTE:** bbb's dSIZE is past the few-tenths bar that makes a row comparable.
It is the loosest match of the four and also the slowest, so read that row as an
upper bound.

## Equal cores

The 1080p rows put both encoders at 15 to 16 cores. The standing table puts us
at 8.5 against x264's 5.9 on foreman, which makes occupancy a candidate for the
entire gap. So: same clip, same 6-second window, auto threads against one
thread.

| clip | threads | wall | work | cores (ours/x264) |
|---|---|--:|--:|---|
| perseverance_1080p | auto | 1.39x | 1.44x | 15.3 / 14.8 |
| perseverance_1080p | 1 | 1.30x | 1.30x | 1.0 / 1.0 |
| perseverance_720p | auto | 1.26x | 1.40x | 14.8 / 13.3 |
| perseverance_720p | 1 | 1.24x | 1.24x | 1.0 / 1.0 |
| foreman_cif | auto | 1.02x | 1.49x | 8.5 / 5.9 |
| foreman_cif | 1 | 1.16x | 1.16x | 1.0 / 1.0 |

Two findings, and the second one surprised me.

On equal cores we are slower than x264 everywhere at the SIMD tier, ranging from
1.16x on foreman to 1.30x at 1080p. Nothing tested reaches parity once both
sides get the same thread count. foreman makes it plainest: hand both encoders
the whole machine and its ratio moves from 1.02x to 1.16x, the wrong way. The
sub-parity CIF rows on the standing table are what filling more cores than x264
looks like.

Threading also costs us more CPU than it costs x264. Every pair's work ratio
rises when the threads come on, by 0.14 at 1080p and by 0.33 on foreman, so our
parallel path burns CPU that theirs does not. CIF is the worst of the three,
which fits a wavefront with the fewest rows to fill.

That splits the standing #1 priority into three deficits instead of one. The
algorithm is fine: the pure-C single-thread tier reads 0.91x to 1.04x on these
same clips. Our SIMD trails x264's hand assembly on one thread. Our parallel
overhead is a third thing, separate from both, and I suspect it is the cheapest
of the three to attack, though nobody has costed it yet.

## Caveats

The CRF-25 anchor here is not the calibrated per-clip operating point the
standing table uses, so these rows don't compare with
`docs/board-2026-08-27.md` cell for cell. The pattern does compare: foreman
reads about 1.4x work at either operating point while its wall ratio sits near
parity.

dVMAF moves with the window. perseverance_720p changes sign between the
15-second window and the 6 seconds inside it, so neither reading is a quality
result on its own.

`touchdown_420` supports no BD claim, and the perseverance and bbb windows are
timed rather than scored. Speed and a matched dSIZE are all any of them are good
for. See `docs/corpus-sources.md`.

## The clips this added

`perseverance_1080p` and `perseverance_720p`, 450 frames each: NASA/JPL-Caltech,
public domain, cut from the SVS 31250 descent video at its highest-motion
cut-free window. Provenance and the rule the window was picked by are in
`docs/corpus-sources.md`. Both come from already-compressed H.264, same as bbb.

## Toolchain changes behind this run

The library rename reached the ffmpeg side. The fork branch is `yah264`, the
encoder is `libyah264`, and the install prefix is `/tmp/y264inst`. The rebuild
recipe in `docs/ffmpeg-integration-plan.md` now includes `--enable-gpl`, which
libx264 requires and the old note omitted. The `next264` branch is still on the
remote, so clone by branch name rather than taking the default.
