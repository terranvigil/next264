Part one · common to every codec

How video encoding works

The same loop runs in x264, in an AV1 encoder, and in the silicon on your phone. Learn it once here; part two is only what H.264 does differently.

Scroll. The panel on the right follows along.
Section one

The problem is the size of the problem

One second of uncompressed 1080p, 4:2:0, at 60 frames a second, is 187 megabytes — 1.5 gigabits per second for a single stream.

A good 1080p60 stream is about 6 Mbit/s. The encoder's job is to discard 249 of every 250 bits and have you not notice. Everything that follows is a way of choosing which.

Section two

Four kinds of redundancy

Compression is bookkeeping against four separate wastes. Every standardised codec attacks the same four.

  • Spatial. Neighbouring pixels look alike, so predict a block from its decoded neighbours and code only the difference.
  • Temporal. Frame 41 is mostly frame 40, moved. Send the motion, not the picture.
  • Statistical. What survives prediction is mostly zero. Spend fractions of a bit on the common values.
  • Perceptual. Eyes weight brightness over colour and gradients over texture. Spend bits where they are seen.

The first three are free. The fourth is an opinion about human vision, and it is where encoders genuinely differ.

Section three

The loop every block goes around

Predict, subtract, transform, quantise, entropy code. Then the encoder decodes its own output and keeps that reconstruction, because it is the only thing the decoder will have to predict from.

Get that return path wrong by one bit and the two sides drift apart a few frames later. This is why conformance is tested on reconstruction, exactly, not on how it looks.

Section four

Exactly one step is lossy

Prediction, transform and entropy coding are all reversible. Only quantisation throws information away: divide each coefficient by a step, round it, move on. QP picks the step, and the step doubles every six QP.

The panel is running a real 8×8 DCT over every block, in your browser. Drag the slider and watch bits collapse faster than fidelity — that gap is the entire business model of video.

Notice how it fails: in 8×8 squares, the transform's own grid. That is why a deblocking filter sits inside the loop of every codec since.

Section five

I, P and B

Not every frame can lean on a neighbour, and the ones that can needn't only look backwards. Frames leave the encoder out of display order so a B-frame can see the future — which is exactly the reordering live encoding cannot always afford.

Section six

Rate control is a policy, not a knob

Quantisation gives one knob per block. Rate control sets it thousands of times a second, blind to the future, against a target it may not miss.

  • CQP — fix it and let the bitrate land where it lands.
  • CRF — hold quality steady, let bitrate move. The sane default.
  • ABR — hit an average across the file.
  • VBV — never overflow the decoder's buffer. Broadcast and ABR streaming require it.

An encoder that has seen the next fifty frames knows the quiet shot is about to cut to an explosion, and starts saving. That is what a lookahead buys.

Section seven

Measuring it honestly

PSNR measures squared error. SSIM looks at structure. VMAF is fitted to human ratings and is the closest to useful, with its own blind spots.

Comparing encoders takes a curve, not a number. BD-rate integrates the gap between two rate/quality curves: −5% is the same quality for 5% fewer bits across the measured range. It is half a comparison — always read it beside the time it cost.
