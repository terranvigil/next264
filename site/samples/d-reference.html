How video encoding works

Every codec solves the same problem the same way. Learn the shape once and the H.264-specific machinery in part two reads as a list of choices rather than a wall of acronyms.

The bit budget

A second of uncompressed 1080p, 4:2:0, at 60 frames a second is 187 MB — 1.5 gigabits per second. A good stream of the same content is about 6 Mbit/s. The encoder discards 249 of every 250 bits and its whole craft is choosing which ones.

Four redundancies

Compression attacks four separate wastes. These four terms recur on every page of this documentation, and they keep their colours throughout.

spatial
Neighbouring pixels look alike. Predict a block from pixels already decoded above and to the left, then code only the difference.
temporal
Frame 41 is mostly frame 40, displaced. Find where each block went and send the motion vector instead of the picture.
statistical
What survives prediction is mostly zeroes and small numbers. An entropy coder spends fractions of a bit on the common values and more on the rare ones.
perceptual
Vision weighs brightness over colour, and smooth gradients over detail inside texture. Spend the bits where they will be noticed.

The first three are bookkeeping and cost nothing in quality. Only perceptual is a judgement call, which is why two encoders at one bitrate are mostly disagreeing about vision.

The encode loop

Every block runs the same circuit: predict, subtract, transform, quantise, entropy code. Then the encoder decodes its own output and keeps the result — the decoder will never have anything better to predict from.

The loop, in order

predict → subtract → transform → quantise → entropy code → bits out

inverse quantise → inverse transform → reconstruct → deblock → reference for the next frame

Get the second row wrong by one bit and encoder and decoder drift apart within a few frames. That is why conformance is tested on reconstruction, exactly, and never on appearance.

Quantisation, the only lossy step

Prediction, transform and entropy coding are reversible. Quantisation is not: divide each coefficient by a step size, round, discard the remainder. QP selects the step, which doubles every six QP.

Rate control

Quantisation gives one knob per block. Rate control is the policy that sets it thousands of times a second, blind to the future, against a target it must not miss.

  • CQP — fix the knob; bitrate lands where it lands.
  • CRF — hold quality steady and let bitrate move. The sane default.
  • ABR — hit an average over the file; needs a lookahead.
  • VBV — never overflow the decoder's buffer; required by broadcast and ABR streaming.

Measuring it

PSNR measures squared error, SSIM structure, and VMAF is fitted to human ratings. Comparing encoders needs a curve rather than a number: BD-rate integrates the gap between two rate/quality curves, so −5% means the same quality for 5% fewer bits across the measured range. It is half of a comparison; the other half is what it cost in time.
