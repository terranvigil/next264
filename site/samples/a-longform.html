Fundamentals · common to every codec

How video encoding works

Before any of the H.264-specific machinery makes sense, it helps to know what every video encoder is doing and why. This page has no H.264 in it at all.

Part one of the yah264 documentation. Nothing here is specific to our encoder — the same loop runs in x264, in an AV1 encoder, and in the hardware block on your phone.

The bit budget

A second of uncompressed 1080p, 4:2:0, at 60 frames a second, is about 187 MB. Written out: 1920 × 1080 samples of luma, half that again for the two chroma planes, one byte each, times sixty. That is 1.5 gigabits per second for a single stream.

A good 1080p60 stream lands around 6 Mbit/s. So the encoder's job, stated bluntly, is to throw away 249 out of every 250 bits and have you not notice. Everything below is a technique for choosing which ones.

Four kinds of redundancy

Compression is not magic, it is bookkeeping against four separate kinds of waste. Every codec ever standardised attacks the same four.

  • Spatial. Neighbouring pixels look alike. A patch of sky is thousands of samples saying almost the same number. Predict a block from the pixels already decoded above and to its left, and you only have to code the difference.
  • Temporal. Consecutive frames look alike. Most of frame 41 is frame 40, moved. Find where each block went — motion estimation — and send a vector instead of a picture.
  • Statistical. After prediction, the numbers left over are mostly small and mostly zero. An entropy coder spends fractions of a bit on the common values and more on the rare ones.
  • Perceptual. Your eye is far more sensitive to brightness than to colour, and to smooth gradients than to detail inside texture. A codec spends its bits where they will be seen.

Only the last one is lossy by choice

The first three are bookkeeping: done well, they cost nothing in quality. The fourth is a judgement call about human vision, and it is where encoders differ most from each other. Two encoders at the same bitrate are, mostly, disagreeing about perception.

The encode loop

Every block of every frame goes around the same circuit. Predict it, subtract the prediction, transform what is left, quantise, entropy code. Then — and this is the part people miss — the encoder decodes its own output and keeps the result, because the decoder will only ever have that version to predict from.

Where the quality actually goes

Prediction and transform are reversible. Entropy coding is reversible. Exactly one step throws information away: quantisation, dividing every transform coefficient by a step size and rounding. The step size is what a QP number selects, and in every modern codec it doubles every six QP.

Two things to notice. Bits fall away much faster than fidelity at first — that is the whole business model of video compression. And when it does break, it breaks in 8×8 squares, because that is the grid the transform works on. Blocking artefacts are quantisation showing through the transform's own seams, which is why every codec since has bolted a deblocking filter onto the loop.

I, P and B

Not every frame can be predicted from a neighbour, and the ones that can needn't all look backwards.

TypePredicts fromCosts
Inothing outside itselfthe most bits; the only place you can start decoding
Pearlier framesfar less; carries motion vectors and a residual
Bearlier and later framesleast of all; requires reordering, adds delay

Sending frames out of display order is what lets a B-frame see the future. The cost is latency and bookkeeping, which is why live encoding often gives B-frames up.

Rate control

Quantisation gives you one knob per block. Rate control is the policy that sets it — thousands of times a second, without being able to see the future, against a target you must not miss.

  • Constant QP — fix the knob, let the bitrate go where it goes. Useful for experiments and almost nothing else.
  • Constant quality (CRF) — hold perceived quality steady and let the bitrate move. The right default when file size is negotiable.
  • Average bitrate — hit a number over the whole file. Needs a lookahead to know whether the hard part is still coming.
  • Buffer-constrained (VBV) — never overflow a decoder's buffer. What broadcast and adaptive streaming actually require.

Why lookahead earns its keep

An encoder that has seen the next fifty frames knows that this quiet shot is about to cut to an explosion, and can start saving. One that hasn't will spend its budget on the quiet part and starve the part you were watching for.

Measuring it

You cannot improve what you cannot measure, and video quality metrics are all wrong in interesting ways. PSNR measures squared error and mildly disagrees with human eyes. SSIM looks at structure. VMAF is a learned model trained on human ratings, and is the closest to useful, with its own blind spots.

Comparing two encoders needs more than one number, because quality and bitrate trade against each other. BD-rate integrates the gap between two rate/quality curves: −5% BD-rate means the same quality for 5% fewer bits across the measured range. It is half of a comparison, not a headline — always read it beside the speed it cost.
