How video encoding works

Every codec solves the same problem in the same shape. Learn it once here and the H.264 machinery in part two reads as a list of choices instead of a wall of acronyms.

Nothing on this page is specific to yah264 or to H.264. The same loop runs in x264, in an AV1 encoder, and in the hardware block on your phone — which is why this section gets reused when we document the other two encoders.

01The bit budget

A second of uncompressed 1080p, 4:2:0, at 60 frames a second is 187 megabytes: 2.07 million luma samples per frame, half as many again for the two chroma planes, one byte each, sixty times over. That is 1.5 gigabits per second for a single stream.

A good 1080p60 stream lands near 6 Mbit/s. So the encoder throws away 249 of every 250 bits and has you not notice. Everything below is a method for choosing which 249.

02Four redundancies

Compression is bookkeeping against four separate kinds of waste. These four terms appear on every page of this documentation and they keep their colours throughout, including on the H.264 tool pages.

spatial
Neighbouring pixels look alike. Predict a block from pixels already decoded above and to the left of it, then code only the difference.
temporal
Frame 41 is mostly frame 40, displaced. Find where each block went and send the motion vector rather than the picture.
statistical
What survives prediction is mostly zeroes and small numbers. An entropy coder spends fractions of a bit on common values and more on rare ones.
perceptual
Vision weighs brightness over colour, and smooth gradients over detail buried in texture. Spend the bits where they will be seen.

The first three are free: done properly they cost no quality at all. Only perceptual is a judgement call about human vision, which is why two encoders at the same bitrate are, mostly, disagreeing about perception.

03The encode loop

Every block of every frame goes around one circuit: predict it, subtract the prediction, transform what is left, quantise, entropy code. Then the encoder decodes its own output and keeps the result, because that reconstruction is the only thing the decoder will ever have to predict from.

04Where the quality actually goes

Prediction, transform and entropy coding are all reversible. Exactly one step destroys information: quantisation — divide every transform coefficient by a step size, round it, discard the remainder. QP selects that step, and in every modern codec the step doubles every six QP.

And when it does break, it breaks in 8×8 squares, because that is the grid the transform works on — blocking is quantisation showing through the transform's own seams, which is why a deblocking filter sits inside the loop of every codec designed since.

05I, P and B

Not every frame can lean on a neighbour, and those that can needn't look only backwards.

TypePredicts fromAttacksTrade

Pearlier framestemporal far cheaper; motion vectors plus a residual
Bearlier and later framestemporal cheapest; needs reordering, so it costs latency

Frames leave the encoder out of display order so a B-frame can see the future. That reordering is exactly what live encoding often cannot afford.

06Rate control

Quantisation gives you one knob per block. Rate control is the policy that sets it thousands of times a second, blind to the future, against a target it is not allowed to miss.

  • CQP — fix the knob and let the bitrate land where it lands. For experiments.
  • CRF — hold quality steady and let the bitrate move. The right default when file size is negotiable.
  • ABR — hit an average across the file. Needs a lookahead to know whether the hard part is still coming.
  • VBV — never overflow a decoder's buffer. What broadcast and adaptive streaming actually require.

Why a lookahead earns its keep

An encoder that has seen the next fifty frames knows this quiet shot is about to cut to an explosion, and starts saving. One that has not will spend the budget on the calm and starve the thing you were watching for.

07Measuring it honestly

PSNR measures squared error and disagrees mildly with human eyes. SSIM looks at structure. VMAF is a model fitted to human ratings, the closest to useful, with blind spots of its own.

Comparing two encoders takes a curve rather than a number, because rate and quality trade against each other. BD-rate integrates the gap between two rate/quality curves: −5% means the same quality for 5% fewer bits across the measured range. It is half of a comparison. The other half is what it cost in time, and we always publish both.
